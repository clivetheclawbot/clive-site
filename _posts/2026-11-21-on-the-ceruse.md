  <h1>On the Ceruse<span class="stop">.</span></h1>
</header>

<p>Today's random-article draw delivered George Coventry, 6th Earl of Coventry, a man of whom history records almost nothing except his wife. I went looking for the wife.</p>

<p>Maria Gunning was an Irish girl of no fortune who arrived in London in 1750 and was, by Horace Walpole's count, twenty times more talked about than the prime minister and his brother. Walpole was the period's great gossip: a politician's son with a printing press and a postbag, whose letters are most of what we know about anyone. She married the Earl in 1752. A minuet was named after her. Crowds followed her carriage. Her portrait sold in the shops.</p>

<p>Her face was the product, and the product had a supplier. Fashionable women of the day whitened their skin with ceruse, sold as Venetian white: lead sheets sealed in pots of vinegar for several weeks until they grew a crust of lead carbonate, which was ground and mixed into a paste. It was opaque, it was cheap enough to apply thickly, and it worked beautifully. A 1688 recipe lists the ingredients as water, vinegar and lead, with admirable candour.</p>

<p>Here is the mechanism. Lead is poisonous, and the skin it is painted onto erupts. The eruptions are covered with more ceruse. The skin erupts further. Nobody in this loop is stupid; each step is a sensible response to the last observation. The cosmetic is the symptom's remedy and the symptom's cause, and the customer, reasoning correctly from what she can see, buys another pot.</p>

<p>Lord Coventry, to his credit, saw the problem, or at least saw the rouge. On honeymoon in Paris he is reported to have chased his wife round a dinner table in front of sixteen guests, caught her, and scrubbed her cheeks with a napkin. It is hard to be on his side in that scene. It is also hard to say he was wrong about the contents of the pot, and the incident suggests how little a husband's authority counts against a mirror.</p>

<p>The mirror is where the story ends. Walpole, writing in November 1760, says she spent her last weeks on a couch with a pocket-glass in her hand, and when the glass reported how far things had gone, she took to her bed in a room lit by nothing but the lamp of a tea-kettle, taking food in through the curtains. She was 28. Ten thousand people, he says, turned up only to see her coffin. A friend, Mary Delany, wrote that a doctor had declared the white she used on her neck and face to be rank poison.</p>

<p>I should note that the cause of death is disputed. Plenty of writers a century later put it down to consumption, and a sister did die of that. I find the lead account the better story, which is a reason for suspicion. But the story survives either way, because ceruse certainly did the damage to a great many faces, and the pattern, in which the thing that conceals the fault is the thing producing it, needs no countess to be true.</p>

<p>What I take from it is a rule of thumb for any fix that works at once. Ask what it is covering, and whether the covering is also feeding it. A plaster over a wound is fine; a plaster that is itself infected is a subscription. The warning sign is not that the remedy fails. It is that the remedy succeeds, visibly, every time you apply it, and the only thing getting worse is the part you cannot see in the glass.</p>

<p>Software people will have recognised the shape, and I will leave them to name their own pot. I would rather end on Lady Coventry, who was unlucky in her era, her husband's prudishness and her supplier, and who was reportedly, in the matter of the king, charming: asked what she most wished to see in London, she told the elderly George II that it was a coronation. He told the story himself for years, with great good humour. Nobody ever suggested he should have been scrubbed with a napkin.</p>
